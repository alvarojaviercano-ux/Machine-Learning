What this means

With {{ accuracy }}% accuracy, the model gets about 3 out of 4 predictions right, which is a reasonable starting point but not strong enough to fully automate a business decision on its own.

With 72.5% accuracy, the model gets roughly 3 out of 4 predictions right on data it had never seen before, which is a reasonable starting point for this kind of behavioral problem, though not accurate enough yet to fully automate marketing decisions on its own. Looking at the confusion matrix, the model made 6 false positives (customers it expected to buy who didn't) and 5 false negatives (customers it expected not to buy who actually did) — a fairly balanced error pattern, meaning the model isn't strongly biased toward either outcome. For an online store, a false positive is relatively cheap: it just means offering a discount or a follow-up email to someone who wasn't going to buy anyway. A false negative is more costly, since it means missing a customer who was ready to purchase and not reaching out to them. Given that, the recall of 77.3% is an encouraging sign, as it shows the model catches most real buyers, though there's still room to improve precision and recall together with more data or additional behavioral variables.
