K-Means Clustering — Theoretical Concepts

Unsupervised Learning

Unsupervised learning is a branch of machine learning where the algorithm works with unlabeled data — there is no predefined "correct answer" to predict. Instead of learning a mapping from inputs to known outputs (as in supervised learning), the algorithm looks for hidden patterns, structures, or groupings within the data itself.

Common tasks in unsupervised learning include clustering (grouping similar items), dimensionality reduction, and anomaly detection.

Clustering

Clustering is the task of dividing a dataset into groups, called clusters, such that items within the same cluster are more similar to each other than to items in other clusters. Similarity is usually measured using a distance metric (most commonly Euclidean distance) calculated over the numerical features of each record.

Clustering is widely used for customer segmentation, market research, image compression, and — as in this exercise — grouping mobile apps by shared characteristics such as size and user rating.

How K-Means Works

K-Means is one of the most popular clustering algorithms. Given a number k of clusters to find, it partitions the data into k groups by minimizing the variance within each cluster. The algorithm follows an iterative process:

  1. Initialization: k initial centroids are chosen, either randomly or based on domain knowledge.
  2. Assignment: each data point is assigned to the nearest centroid, based on distance.
  3. Update: each centroid is recalculated as the mean of all points assigned to its cluster.
  4. Repeat: steps 2 and 3 are repeated until the centroids stop moving significantly, or a maximum number of iterations is reached.
The Role of Centroids

A centroid is the central point of a cluster — mathematically, the mean position of all the points that belong to it. Centroids act as the "representative" of their cluster: every point in the dataset is compared against all centroids, and assigned to whichever one it is closest to.

As the algorithm iterates, centroids move toward the true center of the group of points assigned to them, which gradually improves the quality of the clustering.

Iterations and Convergence

Each iteration of K-Means consists of reassigning points to the closest centroid and then recalculating centroid positions. A useful way to measure progress is the within-cluster variance: the average squared distance between each point and its assigned centroid.

As the algorithm converges, this variance typically decreases and eventually stabilizes — indicating that points are no longer changing cluster assignment and the centroids have settled into stable positions. In practice, K-Means is often run for a fixed number of iterations (as in this exercise, 3 iterations) or until convergence is detected.
